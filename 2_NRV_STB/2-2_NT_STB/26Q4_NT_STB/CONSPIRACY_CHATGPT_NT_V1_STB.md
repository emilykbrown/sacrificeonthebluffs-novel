Yeah. I think this **can absolutely become the small-town conspiracy**, and I think the key is changing what you mean by “conspiracy.”

It doesn't necessarily need to be *a secret organization whose members systematically murder disabled people*. In fact, I think that version risks getting you right back to the organ-harvesting problem: it's huge and horrifying, but the mechanism starts feeling detached from the ableism you actually want the book to be about.

Your much stronger idea is:

> **The conspiracy is the system that makes the deaths possible, excuses them afterward, and eventually learns how to benefit from them.**

That gives you the tangled fucking web you're looking for.

### Start with the smallest version

Decades ago, there is **one death**.

A caregiver kills a disabled family member. Maybe it's a child. Maybe it's an adult dependent on family support. Maybe officially it's an accident, suicide, medical complication, or murder-suicide attempt.

And the town knows—or at least several powerful people know—that something isn't right.

But look at how easy it is to rationalize doing nothing.

The caregiver was exhausted. The family had no support. The victim was profoundly disabled. Their life was "so difficult." The caregiver had "sacrificed everything." The sheriff doesn't want to destroy what remains of the family. A doctor talks about how medically complicated the victim was. A pastor talks about forgiveness. The newspaper writes a sympathetic story about the caregiver's impossible burden.

Nobody has to sit around a table saying:

> *We should murder disabled people.*

That's much scarier.

They tell themselves:

> *This was a tragedy. There are no villains here.*

Except there **was** a victim.

And once the town successfully makes that victim disappear from their own murder story, you've established the mechanism.

### Then it happens again.

Five years later, another disabled person dies under suspicious circumstances.

And somebody involved in the first case recognizes the pattern.

This time there are already precedents for what to do.

Don't investigate too aggressively.

Don't upset the grieving parent.

Don't ask why the medication count doesn't make sense.

Don't publish *that* detail.

Don't interview the victim's disabled friends.

Don't let the state start poking around the local disability program.

And most importantly:

**Protect the town.**

Now you've gone from individual prejudice to institutional behavior.

And over decades, *that becomes the conspiracy.*

Not fifty murders. Not an underground organ market.

Maybe you're right: **there have only been five or six deaths.**

But there have been dozens of people who helped bury those deaths.

That's fucking huge.

### And this is where the money can enter without becoming the whole motive.

I wouldn't make the original killing about money.

I'd make the **cover-up eventually become financially necessary**.

Suppose the town has some institution connected to disability: a residential program, special school, medical facility, nonprofit, respite organization, rehabilitation center, disability charity, county program, whatever ultimately fits your setting.

It's one of the town's biggest employers.

It receives grants.

It brings families into town.

Local politicians brag about it.

Businesses depend on it.

Maybe the entire town has built part of its identity around being *wonderful to disabled people*.

That last part is especially juicy.

Because now you have a town that is **proud of how compassionate it is toward disabled people while disabled people are quietly telling each other that something is deeply fucking wrong.**

And Hannah knows the difference because she's one of the people everyone is supposedly helping.

The money doesn't have to cause the murders.

**The money causes the town to protect the system that enabled them.**

An investigation doesn't just threaten one caregiver. It threatens doctors who ignored warning signs, social workers who falsified something, cops who didn't investigate, administrators who suppressed complaints, politicians who protected the institution, and potentially millions in funding.

Suddenly:

> "We can't destroy this entire community because of one tragic family."

becomes

> "We can't destroy this entire community because of five tragic families."

And nobody wants to ask why this particular community has so many "tragic families."

That's your conspiracy.

### And holy shit, does this give you a red herring.

Because Hannah and the gang can discover that **disabled people keep dying/disappearing around the same institution.**

Naturally, they assume:

**THE INSTITUTION IS KILLING PEOPLE.**

And there's evidence!

Missing records.

Altered files.

People refusing to talk.

A doctor involved in multiple cases.

Cops shutting down questions.

A former employee who abruptly left town.

Parents behaving strangely.

A sealed lawsuit.

A local reporter who stopped investigating.

Maybe there's even an abandoned building / creepy facility / local legend / place teenagers aren't supposed to go—your equivalent of Borrasca's ominous mythology.

Everything points toward some enormous centralized operation.

Except they're wrong.

There isn't one murderer.

**There are several.**

And that's worse.

Different caregivers, different decades, different circumstances.

What connects them isn't *who killed them.*

It's **who decided the disabled victim didn't matter enough to investigate.**

That is incredibly close to the theme you've already written.

### And this solves another problem: the caregivers don't all have to be the same.

This matters enormously because you don't want the book accidentally saying "caregivers are secretly monsters."

One caregiver genuinely is at the end of their rope after being failed catastrophically by every support system around them—and commits an unforgivable act.

Another is abusive and uses caregiver burnout as an excuse.

Another wants money.

Another has bought completely into the idea that death is preferable to disability and genuinely calls what they did "mercy."

Another case might **not actually be murder at all**, which complicates the gang's theory.

And you can have many caregivers who are loving, exhausted, furious at the system, and would *never fucking kill the person they care for.*

That actually strengthens your point:

**lack of support explains desperation. It doesn't turn murder into an inevitable response to disability.**

The town is the thing conflating those ideas.

### And Hannah is perfect for exposing it.

Because imagine how many times she hears some variation of:

*"You wouldn't understand what these parents went through."*

Except Hannah understands something the adults discussing the case don't.

**She understands what it's like to be the disabled person they're talking about.**

She understands needing help.

She understands watching somebody get exhausted helping you.

She understands internalized guilt about being "too much."

She understands people praising caregivers for basic acts while barely acknowledging the disabled person sitting beside them.

And she understands something horrifying:

If her own caregiver killed her, **these people might sympathize with the person who killed her.**

Now your conspiracy isn't something Hannah happens to discover.

It's intimately connected to **how Hannah understands her own place in the world.**

That's the piece you felt was missing from *Borrasca*.

The conspiracy and character story become inseparable.

And I think your duology idea becomes *much* stronger under this version.

**Book One is essentially: Who are these kids to each other?**

Hannah, Gavin, Lauren, Ingrid, whoever ultimately remains in the gang. They're assholes to each other. They love each other. They fight. They make filthy jokes. They know exactly how to help one another without turning each other into inspirational disability stories. Hannah and Gavin's bond can be enormously important. Lauren gets enough page time that the reader actually knows and loves her. Ingrid/Hannah can develop slowly.

Meanwhile, something is wrong with the town.

At first it's background noise.

Then somebody disappears/dies.

Then there are inconsistencies.

Then the gang starts digging.

Then they find something enormous—or what **looks** enormous.

And eventually Hannah's own family becomes implicated.

That's where the found-family premise really pays off:

**The people Hannah is supposed to be able to trust are part of the machinery threatening her, while the bunch of fucked-up disabled kids she's accumulated around herself become the people she can actually trust.**

Then you break that safety apart near the end.

Hannah leaves.

The group fractures.

Lauren is missing.

Whatever happens between Hannah and Gavin destroys what had previously seemed unbreakable.

Book One can end without explaining the whole conspiracy because the emotional catastrophe **is the climax.**

Then Book Two asks:

**What the fuck actually happened here?**

Hannah comes back—or is dragged back into it. Ingrid contacts her. Lauren becomes central. Old friendships have scars. Hannah/Gavin have unresolved shit. Hannah/Ingrid's relationship changes. The characters have to put themselves back together enough to investigate.

And *then* you reveal the history.

One death.

Then another.

Then another.

Five or six over decades.

Different killers.

Same excuses.

Same institutions.

Same cops.

Same doctors.

Same language.

Same fucking town.

And somewhere along the way, Hannah realizes they've been asking the wrong question.

Not:

**"Who's killing disabled people?"**

But:

**"Why does this town keep helping people get away with killing disabled people?"**

I think *that* may be the version of "cerebral palsy Borrasca" you've been trying to find.

Because the horror isn't an unrelated atrocity hidden underneath an otherwise normal town.

**The horror is the town's ableism taken to its logical extreme.**

And that gives you the tangled web you wanted: the murders, lack of services, caregiver burnout, institutional reputation, money, disability inspiration narratives, caregiver sainthood, police failures, Hannah's family, the found family, and Hannah's own fear of being treated as a burden can all be different strands of the **same fucking web** instead of separate themes you have to somehow glue together.

I think this is actually a really good time to do that, because you're rediscovering what the story itself is about. You don't have to preserve Hannah/Gavin/Lauren/Ingrid's old dynamics just because they've been around for years.

Especially now that the core is shifting toward **disabled found family + caregiver killings + a town that protects/excuses those killings**, the relationships can be designed to collide directly with that premise.

I'd separate the decisions into a few buckets rather than trying to rebuild everybody simultaneously:

* **Hannah's central relationship:** Who is her absolute person at the beginning? Who does she trust most? Is that the same person she eventually falls for?
* **Romance:** Friends-to-lovers and enemies-to-lovers create *very* different versions of the duology. You could even have one apparent romantic trajectory in Book 1 before another becomes important.
* **The friendship that breaks:** You already loved the idea of establishing an incredibly strong Hannah/Gavin bond and then wrecking it. You can keep that emotional function without necessarily keeping the old specifics.
* **The conspiracy connection:** Which member has family involved? Which one becomes suspicious first? Which one desperately doesn't want the theory to be true? Which one has already experienced something the others don't understand yet?
* **Lauren's role:** If her disappearance/what happened to her helps propel Book 2, she needs enough independent characterization in Book 1 that she doesn't become "the missing friend everyone cares about." She should have relationships with the others that don't run exclusively through Hannah.
* **Group chemistry:** Who are the two idiots together? Who fights constantly? Who gets vicious when scared? Who can make Hannah laugh when she's absolutely fucking miserable? Who knows when the jokes need to stop? Who has history before the group forms?

And there's one possibility I'd seriously play with before deciding anything: **don't make Hannah the center of every relationship.**

If it's truly a found family, Gavin and Lauren might be extremely close. Ingrid might initially trust Gavin more than Hannah. Lauren and Ingrid might have their own bizarre running jokes. Two characters might have known each other years before Hannah entered the picture. Someone might privately go to someone *other than Hannah* when they're falling apart.

Then when the group fractures, it isn't simply "Hannah loses her friends."

It's a **family splitting into pieces**, with people caught between people they love.

That would make your Book 1 relationship-heavy approach hurt like absolute hell when everything finally goes wrong—and make rebuilding some version of that family in Book 2 matter just as much as solving the conspiracy.

Yeah, I think both of those are worth treating as **open variables** while you're rebuilding the story rather than things you need to preserve from the old draft.

**Changing Hannah and Lauren's names makes sense for two completely different reasons.** If "Hannah" keeps pulling your brain toward a real person, that's enough reason to replace it. And Gavin/Lauren do have a mild sound-alike problem: both are two syllables, have that strong middle/ending *-in/-en* sound, and even look somewhat similar on the page. It's not disastrous, but if you're already reconsidering Lauren, I'd change it.

You also don't need to find their permanent names immediately. For development, you could literally have:

> Hannah → **MC** for now
> Lauren → **Friend 2** for now
> Gavin → Gavin
> Ingrid → Ingrid

Then let their rebuilt personalities suggest names later. That might actually help separate **2020 Hannah** from whoever this protagonist becomes in the new version.

And I REALLY like reopening the time period question, because moving away from the 80s/90s could substantially change the conspiracy in useful ways.

A **modern setting—say late 2010s/2020s—** gives you access to a whole different flavor of ableism. The town doesn't have to openly treat disabled people horribly. In fact, it can publicly be *extremely progressive about disability.*

There's an accessible community center. Disability-awareness fundraisers. Inspirational local-news stories. Caregiver Facebook groups. GoFundMes. TikToks and Instagram accounts about disabled children. A beloved local nonprofit. Politicians talking about inclusion. Parents posting incredibly private details about their disabled kids and being praised for being "warriors."

Meanwhile, the disabled characters are sitting there going:

**You people don't fucking listen to us.**

That fits *beautifully* with what you were writing about caregiver sainthood.

It also gives you a modern version of the small-town secret problem. Smartphones and the internet make it harder to literally make information disappear, **but easier to bury the truth underneath a socially acceptable narrative.**

Someone dies.

The caregiver posts a devastating statement.

There's a fundraiser.

The local news runs the caregiver's story.

Thousands of strangers comment about how nobody could understand what this family endured.

People start attacking anyone who questions what happened.

And somewhere in those comments is a disabled teenager saying, essentially:

**"Why is nobody talking about the person who fucking died?"**

That's incredibly relevant to *your* story.

Technology could also become part of how the gang investigates without magically solving everything. Old Facebook posts contradict official timelines. Somebody screenshots something before it's deleted. They find archived news coverage. Disabled people from outside town start contacting them. Someone's old Tumblr/blog contains information nobody realized mattered. A caregiver has spent ten years publicly documenting their child's life and accidentally created evidence against themselves.

And then you've got the inverse: **the town can weaponize the internet against the kids.**

They're conspiracy theorists.

They're harassing grieving families.

They're troubled teenagers.

The protagonist is "confused."

Gavin has an axe to grind.

Someone digs up an ugly thing one of them posted at sixteen.

Suddenly the group doesn't merely have to discover the truth. They have to fight over **who gets to define the truth publicly.**

That feels much more connected to your central theme than simply transplanting an 80s/90s-style conspiracy into 2026.

There's also no reason "modern" has to mean **literally 2026**. Something like **2014–2019** could be a sweet spot. Smartphones and social media exist, caregiver blogging/social media culture exists, but you can avoid some of the complications of writing a story set in the exact present. Or you could deliberately choose the 2020s because you want contemporary disability culture to be part of the book.

And changing the period could help you distinguish this from *Borrasca*, too. Instead of asking, **"How do I make my version of that kind of creepy old small-town conspiracy?"**, you get to ask:

**What does a small-town conspiracy look like when everybody has a camera, everybody has Google, and everybody thinks they're one viral post away from exposing the truth?**

Turns out people having access to information doesn't mean they'll believe disabled people.

That's a pretty damn good foundation for this story.
